The Problem & The Solution

During the development of an underwater autonomous drone's Direction of Arrival (DoA) acoustics receiver, my group encountered a severe real-time signal processing bottleneck. To run the spatial profiling MUSIC algorithm successfully, data had to be captured before acoustic reflections (pool echoes) corrupted the signal.

The Conflict: Multipath reflections (pool echoes) arrived at the receiver array in around 300 µs. Meanwhile, traditional digital filtering and ADC sampling workflows on our STM32 microcontroller introduced over 200 µs of software latency. This latency compressed our viable sampling window down to an unreliable 100 µs. Furthermore, sensitive hand-made transducers picked-up massive and random transient noise spikes from the ambient pool water.

The Solution: I engineered a low-latency analog front-end edge detector to detect and latch onto valid 40 kHz sonar pulses within a target threshold of 75 µs, completely bypassing software latency.

System Architecture & Component Selection

The core architecture centers on an active envelope detector topology that extracts the time-domain shape of incoming waveforms to differentiate between large transient noise spikes and true, continuous sonar signals. The configuration charges an integrating RC network whose time constant is mathematically optimized such that the charging capacitor will only charge up to a pre-set threshold voltage if the correct transmitted pulse is received.

Once crossed, a high-speed comparator drives its logic level low, triggering an external hardware interrupt directly on an STM32 GPIO pin. The signal chain is broken into four distinct active stages:

  • Full-Wave Precision Rectifier (Analog Devices AD8277): Incoming signals are as small as peak magnitudes of 200 mV, so normal silicon diodes cannot be used. A single AD8277 chip produces full-wave rectification by implementing an absolute value circuit. The AD8277 is a monolithic dual differential amplifier with eight laser-trimmed matched resistors so no external components are needed. It is still able to function for negative valued inputs despite being in a single-supply operation, which allows it to perform its absolute value function.
  • Op-Amp Limiter Stage (Texas Instruments TLV9052): To ensure the RC network accurately integrates time duration independently of fluctuating input signal amplitudes, the rectified voltages must be normalized. This stage places a 2.7V Zener diode directly within the negative feedback loop of a TLV9052 op-amp to clamp the output cleanly at its breakdown boundary. The TLV9052 was chosen for its exceptional slew rate, high Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBWP), low broadband noise, and ultra-low quiescent current. The amplifier is configured with a 100x (+40dB) gain profile to eliminate clipping distortion on weak inputs and maximize threshold precision for inputs up to 2 V-peak.
  • Lossy RC-Filter Network: Functioning as an active single-pole RC integrator, this block utilizes the second operational amplifier contained within the dual TLV9052 package. The passive R and C component values are strategically computed to guarantee that the integrating capacitor reaches its switching threshold after exactly eight full sinusoidal cycles, preventing sub-threshold, transient noise bursts from triggering false logic changes.
  • High-Speed Comparator Stage (Texas Instruments TLV3021): A TLV3021 high-speed comparator configured with hysteresis is used to latch the output and convert it to a digital signal. The comparator has a built-in push-pull output stage so no additional resistor is needed to connect to an STM32.

Challenges & Tradeoffs

In-Band Environmental Noise: Empirical testing revealed that the acoustic transient noise falls inside the desired frequency band of our Low-Noise Amplifier (LNA), meaning a simple filter would not work.

Amplitudes & Dynamic Range: The transient noise spikes often met or exceeded the peak voltage magnitudes of our true transmitted signal. Additionally, because the drone the receiver is on is autonomous, it experiences a large dynamic signal range as it moves closer to or farther from the acoustic pinger source. This made defining a single threshold voltage that would not trigger false-positive results due to massive noise spikes difficult. The problem was overcome by using the op amp limiting circuit, but in order to produce reliable results for the entire dynamic range a gain of atleast +40dB with minimal distortion is needed.

Accurate Modeling: Modeling the charge/discharge profiles of the integrating capacitor as a function of changing hardware parameters was incredibly complex. By implementing the front-end absolute rectifier and limiting amplifier, it became possible to derive equations that depended on controllable parameters. By normalizing the signal magnitude and manually picking a threshold voltage that allowed eight periods for a given frequency I could isolate a unique RC time constant for the entire dynamic range.

Speed vs. Noise Immunity: While system latency should ideally be minimized well below 100 µs, there is a direct engineering tradeoff: sampling more wave periods means it is even less likely for transient noise to trigger an edge detection. Currently, the design checks for 8 continuous periods. If future structural shielding on the hydrophones can reduce the small oscillating bursts of noise then the validation window can be compressed down to a 25 µs to 40 µs latency target.

PCB Design & Layout Optimization

The REV A development board used 2-layers because it was relatively simple to route, however, to ensure minimal distortion on the limiter circuit I will use 4-layers on REV B and strategic copper ground pours near its input node to reduce fast transient distortion.

Parasitic Capacitance Mitigation: The AD8277 utilizes internal feedback loops driven by its non-inverting input nodes making these traces extremely high-impedance paths. To reduce board-level parasitic capacitance and minimize circuit instability I will insulate these pins with a copper-pullback zone and maintain bare FR4 fiberglass substrate around the high impedance nodes.

General Rules: The second layer of the REV A board is a completely uninterrupted copper ground pour with no signal or power traces being routed on the second layer. Bulk electrolytic and low-ESR ceramic capacitors are used for power supply decoupling, and 0603 imperial unit SMD passive components were used to save on the board size. REV B will have 9 less discrete passive components and 2 less integrated circuit chips than REV A, decreasing board space even more, and using 4-layers will drastically ease the routing for the dual-power supply traces.
